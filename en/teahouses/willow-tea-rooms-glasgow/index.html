Overview

The tea rooms at 217 Sauchiehall Street in Glasgow were opened by Catherine (Kate) Cranston in 1903. They are the most completely surviving of her Glasgow tea-room series. Charles Rennie Mackintosh designed the interiors with Margaret Macdonald, and the building is now known as The Mackintosh Tearooms. In Chinese, the older name "Willow Tea Rooms" is still the more familiar one.

It was not a shop that merely sold tea. It was an Edwardian urban drawing room where women could eat, meet and listen to music without depending on a private house. Tea rooms, exhibition space, a shop and events still share the same building today.

History

In 1903 Cranston commissioned Mackintosh to remodel the Sauchiehall Street premises. Mackintosh took charge of the architecture and furniture, while Margaret Macdonald contributed to the interior decoration. Together they turned line, glass, metal and pale wood into a complete spatial language. The name came from the willow trees behind the building and from the willow motif used at the entrance and inside.

The original scheme contained rooms with different characters: a front saloon facing the street, more intimate saloons and the richly decorated room later called the Salon de Luxe. It is treated as one of Glasgow's earliest contributions to European modernism, not because it was "handsomely decorated" but because it treated a commercial interior as a total work of art.

In the later twentieth century the building and interiors were altered, damaged and sold several times. In 2014 the Glasgow businessman Celia Sinclair bought the building, and the Willow Tea Rooms Trust led a restoration. The work was completed in 2018, when the tea rooms reopened as Mackintosh at the Willow. In 2024 the National Trust for Scotland took over the property to secure its long-term operation.

In February 2026 the National Trust for Scotland renamed it The Mackintosh Tearooms. The official explanation is straightforward: the new name makes the building's relationship with Mackintosh and Margaret Macdonald explicit and places it within Glasgow's central heritage offer. Willow Tea Rooms remains a historical name, but it is no longer the current official one.

Tea and space

The point of the interior is not "period decoration" but Mackintosh's control of proportion and light. The front saloon uses high-backed chairs, screens, mirrors and pale table tops to organise sightlines; the Salon de Luxe uses stronger colour, metal lines and decorative panels for a more intimate mood. From the windows, the Sauchiehall Street frontage is visible, while the interior frames its own views.

The menu is a typical Scottish tea-room combination: loose-leaf tea, coffee, cakes, scones, lunch and afternoon tea. It does not turn tea into a precious tasting ceremony; it puts tea back into everyday social life, with a pot, a plate of cakes and a conversation. Today the trust also runs an exhibition and shop, so the route feels like "heritage site plus restaurant" rather than a pure teahouse.

Visiting

Address: 217 Sauchiehall Street, Glasgow G2 3EX, United Kingdom. Status: operating; the National Trust page listed daily lunch and afternoon tea in 2026. Checked on 25 November 2026.

Afternoon tea, the exhibition and guided visits are usually booked separately; times should be confirmed on the National Trust for Scotland page for the day. If the interior is the main reason for visiting, combine the tearooms, exhibition and surrounding streets in one trip. It is not a place for a quick cup on the way past.
