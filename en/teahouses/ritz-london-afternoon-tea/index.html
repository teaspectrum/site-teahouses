Overview

The Ritz London stands at 150 Piccadilly and opened in 1906. Its afternoon tea is served in the Palm Court, and the hotel markets the experience as “Tea at the Ritz”. This is formal hotel dining, not a standalone teahouse that a visitor can enter at any hour.

The Palm Court is a cream-and-gold Louis XVI room: mirrored panels, gilded oval-backed chairs, chandeliers and a glazed ceiling. Its reputation comes less from a single menu item than from the way the room turns entering the hotel into a ritual.

History

César Ritz, the Swiss hotelier, drove the project; Charles Mewès and Arthur Davis were among the architects, and the hotel opened in 1906. Ritz had already run a hotel in Paris, and he brought to Piccadilly the idea that service should make the guest feel anticipated rather than processed. The Palm Court was originally known as the Winter Garden before it became the tea room; its La Source fountain and mirror scheme still belong to that early layer.

After the First World War, “Tea at the Ritz” became a settled phrase in London social life. Wikipedia records Edward VII, Winston Churchill, Evelyn Waugh and Judy Garland among people associated with the room. Those names show its long role as a meeting place for society and theatre, but this page does not turn each visit into a verifiable private anecdote.

In the 1970s the windows at either end of the Palm Court were replaced with mirror panels. The room became more enclosed and more stage-like. It remains one of the hotel’s most recognisable interiors.

Tea and space

The tea follows the English three-tier sequence: finger sandwiches first, then warm scones with Cornish clotted cream and strawberry preserve, then pastries and cakes. Cakes are cut tableside from a trolley, which gives the service its live, theatrical edge. The official page lists about 20 loose-leaf teas, selected under the hotel’s certified Tea Master; the Ritz Royal Blend is exclusive to the house.

The room speaks before the food does. Daylight comes through the glazed roof; at night the chandeliers and mirrors scatter the light, while a live ensemble keeps a low, arranged hum. It suits anniversaries, family gatherings and formal meetings rather than a quick daily cup.

Visiting

Address: 150 Piccadilly, London W1J 9BR. Sittings are commonly listed at 11.30, 13.30, 15.30, 17.30 and 19.30, and booking is required. The official dress code asks men to wear a jacket and tie and excludes jeans and sportswear for either sex. Prices, seasonal menus and seating are subject to the day’s official information.

Source boundary

This page uses only facts that the hotel website and Wikipedia can support: the 1906 opening, César Ritz and the architects, the Louis XVI Palm Court, its mirrors and fountain, the three-tier tea service, loose-leaf teas and the dress code. Old claims about a royal guest’s fixed table, tea being served from the first day, or a particular “best in the world” award do not form a continuous evidence chain and are not repeated. Prices and tea lists change by season and are not presented as fixed facts.
